VILMA2 · Interactive tutorial

The deforming Earth, in six equations.

VILMA2 solves the coupled viscoelastic and sea-level response of a rotating Earth to a time-varying surface load. This tutorial walks through the six equations at the model's core and the numerical choices behind each.

Preliminaries · Optional

Refreshers and name origins.

Background concepts and the people the terms come from. Expand what you need; skip the rest and continue to § 01.

Diagonalizing a linear operator Refresher

A linear operator L acting on a function space is diagonal in a basis of its eigenfunctions {φk}, meaning L φk = λk φk. Any function expanded in that basis, f = Σk ck φk, transforms as L f = Σk λk ck φk.

The coupled operator equation collapses to independent scalar equations, one per eigenvalue. This is why spherical-harmonic expansion is useful for the load problem: the Laplacian and related operators are diagonal in that basis (see next entry), and each degree evolves on its own.

The Laplacian on a sphere Refresher

On a sphere of radius a, the angular part of the Laplacian has spherical harmonics as its eigenfunctions:

\[ \nabla_\Omega^2\, Y_l^m \;=\; -\,l(l+1)\, Y_l^m. \]

Any surface field expanded in Ylm therefore satisfies elliptic angular problems degree by degree. Radial and horizontal parts remain coupled through boundary conditions but as independent one-dimensional radial ODEs per l.

Real vs complex spherical harmonics Convention

The complex form Ylm ∝ Plm(cos θ) eimφ is complex-valued for m ≠ 0. The real form replaces eimφ with cos(mφ) for m ≥ 0 and sin(|m|φ) for m < 0.

Normalizations vary: fully normalized (unit inner product on the sphere), Schmidt semi-normalized (common in geomagnetism), and 4π-normalized (common in geodesy). Our implementation uses the real, fully normalized convention consistent with SHTns.

Picard fixed-point iteration Refresher

For an equation x = F(x) with F contractive (‖F′‖ < 1), the sequence xk+1 = F(xk) converges to the unique fixed point, with linear rate set by ‖F′‖.

Picard iteration requires only function evaluations, no Jacobian. In the sea-level equation it takes the form: guess S, evaluate the load, apply the Love-number kernels to obtain N and U, update S, and repeat. Convergence is typically reached in three to five iterations.

Green's-function / convolution response Refresher

For a linear time-invariant system, the response to an arbitrary forcing σ(t) is the convolution of the forcing with the system's impulse response G(t):

\[ u(t) \;=\; \int_0^{t} G(t - t')\, \sigma(t')\, dt'. \]

For the viscoelastic load problem, G is a sum of an instantaneous elastic step and exponential relaxation modes (§ 03). The convolution has an exact recursion under trapezoidal integration, so time-stepping the viscoelastic response has no memory-storage cost beyond one mode amplitude per relaxation mode.

Numerical integrator order Refresher

The local truncation error of an integrator scales as Δtp+1, where p is the order. Forward Euler is first-order (error ∝ Δt²) and trapezoidal is second-order (error ∝ Δt³). Over a fixed integration interval, halving Δt reduces the accumulated error by a factor of 2p.

At a fixed error tolerance, the number of steps required scales as tolerance−1/p. This is the source of the ~103 step-count ratio between Forward Euler and trapezoidal that we see on glacial-cycle runs (§ 06).

Load Love numbers Origin

Augustus Edward Hough Love (1863–1940), British mathematician, introduced dimensionless coefficients for the elastic response of a self-gravitating spherical Earth in Some Problems of Geodynamics (Cambridge, 1911). The vertical-displacement number hl and the geoid-perturbation number kl are his; the horizontal-displacement number ll was introduced independently by Toshi Shida in 1912. Peltier (1974) extended the definitions to viscoelastic (load) Love numbers.

Maxwell body Origin

James Clerk Maxwell (1831–1879), Scottish physicist, proposed the series spring-and-damper viscoelastic model in On the Dynamical Theory of Gases (1867) as a description of the internal friction of gases. The "Maxwell time" τM = η/μ is named after him by convention.

Chandler wobble Origin

Seth Carlo Chandler Jr. (1846–1913), American astronomer, reported in 1891 a ~433-day period in the observed variation of the Earth's rotation axis, distinct from the ~365-day annually forced component. The observed period is longer than the ~305-day Euler prediction for a rigid Earth because the real Earth is deformable and the Chandler period includes a viscoelastic correction.

Liouville equation Origin

Joseph Liouville (1809–1882), French mathematician, formulated the general equation for the rotation of a deformable body. The linearized form used for glacial-isostatic true polar wander follows the exposition of Munk and MacDonald (The Rotation of the Earth, 1960).

Farrell–Clark sea-level equation Origin

William E. Farrell derived the elastic-Earth Green's functions for surface loads in 1972. James A. Clark and Farrell extended the framework to the coupled ice–ocean–solid Earth problem in On postglacial sea level (Geophys. J. R. Astron. Soc., 1976). Their integral formulation is the basis for the sea-level equation solved in VILMA2 and most other modern GIA codes.

PREM · Preliminary Reference Earth Model Origin

Adam M. Dziewonski and Don L. Anderson published PREM in 1981 as a one-dimensional radial Earth model constrained by seismic phase arrivals, normal-mode data, and mass and inertia constraints. It gives density and elastic parameters as a function of depth. In VILMA2, PREM supplies the elastic profile and a viscosity structure is overlaid separately.

SHTns · spherical-harmonic transform library Origin

Nathanaël Schaeffer released SHTns (2013) as a highly optimized C library for spherical-harmonic transforms, using fast Legendre-transform algorithms and SIMD-friendly memory layouts. VILMA2 uses SHTns for every forward and inverse transform in the load-response pipeline.

Eustatic, geoid, isostasy Terminology

Eustatic denotes a spatially uniform component of sea-level change; the term is from Eduard Suess, Das Antlitz der Erde (1885). Geoid is the equipotential surface of Earth's gravity field that best matches long-term mean sea level; the name was proposed by J. B. Listing (1873). Isostatic equilibrium is the state in which the mantle load is balanced by buoyancy so that no net vertical stress remains at depth; the fluid limit of the Love-number spectrum corresponds to full isostatic compensation.

01 · Rheology

The mantle as a Maxwell body.

A single constitutive law with two parameters — rigidity μ and viscosity η — spans both the elastic and viscous regimes of the Earth's mantle. Their ratio sets the timescale at which behavior transitions from one to the other.

The mantle responds elastically on timescales shorter than its Maxwell time and viscously on longer ones. A Maxwell body — an elastic spring and a viscous damper (a piston moving through a viscous fluid) connected in series, sharing the same stress — captures both regimes in a single constitutive law.

\[ \dot{\varepsilon} \;=\; \frac{1}{\mu}\, \dot{\sigma} \;+\; \frac{\sigma}{\eta} \] Maxwell

Here ε is strain, σ is deviatoric stress, μ is the elastic rigidity, and η is the viscosity. Under a step in stress the spring contributes an instantaneous strain σ/μ; the damper contributes a viscous strain rate σ/η. The two rates share the same units, so their ratio defines a single characteristic time:

\[ \tau_M \;=\; \frac{\eta}{\mu} \;\approx\; \frac{10^{21}\,\text{Pa·s}}{10^{11}\,\text{Pa}} \;\approx\; 300\ \text{yr}. \]

Loads on timescales below τM see essentially the elastic Earth; loads on timescales above see the fluid limit. Glacial cycles straddle this boundary, so both regimes matter in a single simulation.

The real mantle is a stack of Maxwell bodies, one per radial layer, each with its own (ρ, μ, η). VILMA2 reads this depth profile from a plain-text table selected by the earth parameter.

earth_models/Bagge.datearth_models/M3-L70-V01.datearth="PREM"

Each spherical-harmonic degree l then acquires its own spectrum of relaxation times rather than a single τM. Short-wavelength (high-l) loads stress only the shallow layers and relax on centennial timescales. Long-wavelength (low-l) loads sample the deep mantle and relax on glacial timescales.

02 · Basis

Spherical harmonics diagonalize the Laplacian.

The spherical-harmonic basis is the natural spectral representation on a sphere. In this basis the Laplacian is diagonal, and the coupled elastic-gravitational problem separates into independent radial problems, one per degree.

Any square-integrable function on the sphere admits an expansion in spherical harmonics Ylm, indexed by degree l and order m:

\[ f(\theta, \varphi) \;=\; \sum_{l=0}^{\infty} \sum_{m=-l}^{l} f_{lm}\, Y_l^m(\theta, \varphi). \]

The degree l sets the number of nodal lines and hence the angular scale of the pattern. The order m distributes those nodal lines between latitude and longitude:

  • m = 0 — zonal harmonics with only latitudinal bands.
  • |m| = l — sectoral harmonics with only longitudinal slices.
  • 0 < |m| < l — tesseral harmonics with a mixed nodal pattern.

The utility of this basis follows from a single property of the harmonics on a sphere of radius a:

\[ \nabla^2\, Y_l^m \;=\; -\,\frac{l(l+1)}{a^2}\, Y_l^m. \]

The Laplacian is therefore diagonal, and Poisson's equation, the biharmonic equation for elastic loading, and the geoid perturbation all decouple degree by degree. In our implementation this reduces the three-dimensional load problem to a set of independent one-dimensional radial problems, one per (l, m).

VILMA2 uses the SHTns library for the forward and inverse spherical-harmonic transforms. The truncation degree lmax is a run-time parameter set in the namelist.

&vilma lmax=128SHT_QUICK_INIT=1

Doubling lmax quadruples the number of coefficients — approximately (lmax + 1)² — and multiplies the transform cost by roughly a factor of five. The typical trade-off is:

  • lmax=64 — around 0.8 s per 100 yr of simulation; resolves patterns down to about 300 km.
  • lmax=128 — around 4.4 s per 100 yr; about 150 km resolution, matching VILMA1-parity runs.

The environment variable SHT_QUICK_INIT=1 skips the SHTns internal transform-plan auto-tune, reducing startup from around 136 s to under one second. We set it in production runs and disable it only when benchmarking transform kernels.

  • sht_class — thin wrapper around SHTns, providing forward and inverse transforms.
  • vilma_field — physical fields carry both grid and spectral views, kept in sync on demand.

03 · Response

Load Love numbers, one spectrum per degree.

The response of a spherically symmetric layered Earth to a spherical-harmonic load component of degree l is a sum of exponentials. Three families of coefficients — hl, kl, and ll — together encode the entire radial viscoelastic profile.

The diagonalization from § 2 implies that a load coefficient σlm(t) drives a displacement coefficient ulm(t) that depends on l only, not on m. The response is the sum of an instantaneous elastic step and a superposition of exponential modes:

\[ u_{lm}(t) \;=\; \frac{a}{M_E}\left[\, h_l^E\, \sigma_{lm}(t) \;+\; \sum_j h_l^{(j)} \int_0^{t} e^{-(t-t')/\tau_l^{(j)}}\, \dot{\sigma}_{lm}(t')\, dt' \right]. \]

The coefficients hlE, hl(j), and τl(j) are the load Love numbers. Together they compress the radial viscoelastic profile into a small set of numbers, one set per degree. The fluid limit hlF = hlE + Σj hl(j) gives the isostatic equilibrium response.

Three families are needed. The Love number hl gives the radial displacement, kl gives the induced geoid perturbation, and ll gives the horizontal displacement. For a given radial structure, all three are computed once as functions of l and stored as time-response kernels.

VILMA2 offers three schemes for the viscoelastic response, selected by the earth_scheme parameter:

  • ve — classical normal-mode decomposition. The layered problem is diagonalized into j = 1..Nmodes exponentials per degree. This is the reference implementation and matches VILMA1 to around 0.1 %.
  • fe — a finite-element radial solve driven directly by the load. It is cheaper per step and carries no mode-truncation error, but requires sub-stepping for stability at short τ.

Our default configuration combines fe with a three-dimensional layer split. On standard deglaciation runs it is roughly 111× faster than trapezoidal-ve at comparable accuracy.

Where in the code

  • vilma_response — the finite-element radial solver.
  • ve_response — normal-mode reference implementation.

04 · Sea level

The sea-level equation and its fingerprint.

Sea-level change is not spatially uniform. Removing mass from an ice sheet reduces the local geoid, so relative sea level drops in the near field, while global mass conservation raises it in the far field. The sea-level equation encodes this fingerprint.

Relative sea level S is defined as the difference between the geoid perturbation N and the solid-surface uplift U, plus a spatially uniform term c(t) that enforces mass conservation across the ocean domain 𝒪:

\[ S(\theta, \varphi, t) \;=\; \bigl[\, N(\theta, \varphi, t) \;-\; U(\theta, \varphi, t)\, \bigr]\, \mathcal{O}(\theta, \varphi, t) \;+\; c(t). \]

Both N and U depend on the applied load, and the load itself contains an ocean contribution proportional to S. The system is therefore nonlinear:

\[ \text{load} \;=\; \rho_{\text{ice}}\, H_{\text{ice}} \;+\; \rho_w\, S \quad\longrightarrow\quad N,\ U \quad\longrightarrow\quad S \quad\longrightarrow\quad \text{load}. \]

We solve this by Picard iteration at each time step: given an initial guess for S, evaluate the load, propagate through the Love-number kernels of § 3 to obtain N and U, update S, and repeat until convergence. In practice three to five iterations suffice.

The SLE is solved by Picard fixed-point iteration nested inside every time step. Three implementation details matter for accuracy on the standard Martinec benchmark cases:

  • Sub-grid coastlines. The ocean function 𝒪 is refined below the spherical-harmonic grid resolution, so a partially flooded cell contributes its correct area fraction to the ocean load. This is essential where the load geometry has strong gradients across the coast.
  • Ocean-function updates. Each iteration recomputes 𝒪(θ, φ, t) from the current S before recomputing the load. Skipping this update produces a systematic drift in the far-field amplitude.
  • Coupling iteration and integration order. Iterating the SLE inside the trapezoidal time step is what delivers the ~1300× accuracy gain over Forward Euler at fixed Δt (see § 6). The iteration itself is not the order lever; the trapezoidal quadrature is.

All four Martinec cases (C2, D3, E2, F1) pass to benchmark tolerance in our implementation.

Where in the code

  • vilma_sle — the coupled solve; the advance routine wraps the fixed-point loop.
  • vilma_ocean_function — sub-grid coast mask and ocean-function update.

05 · Rotation

Off-axis mass drives the rotation pole.

A surface mass anomaly off the rotation axis perturbs the Earth's inertia tensor and drives the rotation pole toward the load. The resulting drift is on the order of metres over a glacial cycle, small in absolute terms but non-negligible for sea level at high latitudes.

The rotation axis of a rigid body aligns with its principal axis of maximum moment of inertia. When a surface load perturbs the off-diagonal components of the inertia tensor, the pole drifts toward the new principal axis. For small perturbations the linearized Liouville equation gives the drift:

\[ \frac{d\mathbf{m}}{dt} \;+\; \frac{\mathbf{m}}{T_{\text{CW}}} \;=\; \frac{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}{C - A}. \]

Here m is the pole offset in the equatorial plane, Ψ is the excitation function set by the off-diagonal inertia perturbation of the load, and C − A is the difference between the axial and equatorial moments of inertia. The relaxation time TCW is the Chandler-wobble period modified by viscoelastic relaxation of the deformable Earth.

The pole trajectory is a damped spiral in (mx, my): the pole rotates around the excitation direction while its distance from that direction decays. In our implementation the resulting centrifugal-potential change enters the sea-level equation as a degree-2, order-1 correction to the load, without any new solver.

06 · Time integration

Trapezoidal integration and adaptive time stepping.

The integrator choice dominates the cost of the transient solve. At fixed accuracy, Forward Euler requires roughly three orders of magnitude more time steps than the trapezoidal rule.

The two candidate rules for advancing an ODE dy/dt = f(y, t) from step n to n+1 are Forward Euler,

\[ y_{n+1} \;=\; y_n \;+\; \Delta t\, f(y_n, t_n), \]

and the trapezoidal rule,

\[ y_{n+1} \;=\; y_n \;+\; \frac{\Delta t}{2}\, \bigl[\, f(y_n, t_n) \;+\; f(y_{n+1}, t_{n+1})\, \bigr]. \]

The trapezoidal rule averages the slopes at the two endpoints and is second-order accurate; Forward Euler is first-order. Halving Δt reduces the trapezoidal error by a factor of four but the Forward Euler error by a factor of two. Over a glacial-cycle integration these factors compound, and for our fixed tolerance the ratio in required step count is around 103.

Because yn+1 appears on both sides of the trapezoidal update, the step is implicit and requires an inner solve. In our coupled implementation this solve coincides with the SLE Picard iteration of § 4, so the two are folded into a single fixed-point loop.

The vilma_timestep module implements the adaptive trapezoidal rule with step doubling for error estimation:

  • Advance one full step of size Δt. Advance two half-steps of size Δt/2. The difference between the two solutions estimates the local truncation error.
  • If the estimated error exceeds the tolerance, Δt is reduced and the step is retried. If the error is well under tolerance, Δt is grown.
  • First-step initialization uses σ0 from the load rate, avoiding the classic cold-start blow-up on the first step.

The gain from adaptive stepping depends on the load. On steady forcing, Δt is held at the upper bound and no wall-clock is saved. On loads with a large dynamic range, such as a rapid deglaciation transient, we observe a factor of around 1.6 in wall-clock, because Δt is reduced only where the load is changing rapidly.

Restarts persist the full integrator state — the trial step dt_try, the stored slope σn, and the mode amplitudes. A resumed run is therefore bit-for-bit identical to a continuous one.

On the 111× speed-up The reported factor of around 111 compares the default fe scheme with stability sub-stepping and a three-dimensional layer split against the trapezoidal-ve reference on standard one-dimensional deglaciation runs. Trapezoidal-ve is the more accurate scheme; the fe scheme accepts slightly less accuracy per step in exchange for a substantially lower cost.

  • vilma_timestep — the adaptive-Δt controller with step doubling.
  • solid_earth%update(h_ice, dt) — the top-level advance, with the coupling iteration nested inside.
